II

Where Samhain Originated

Samhain

pronounced SOW-win

A Gaelic word meaning “summer’s end.”

Over 2,000 years ago

The Samhain tradition began with the Celts. They celebrated on November 1, but their days started and ended at sunset, so the celebration began the night of October 31 and lasted until sunset on the 1st.

A new year

The ancient Celts of Gaul, where modern-day France is, celebrated their New Year at Samhain.

It marked the end of summer and the harvest, and led into winter.

First in writing

Samhain is first mentioned in literature around the 9th century, in a tale whose author is unknown.

Tochmarc Emire
The Wooing of Emer

The veil

Samhain wasn’t only about the final harvest of the year. The deceased were a main focus.

The veil between here and the Spirit realm was believed to be at its thinnest. It was, and still is, considered a good time to communicate with the dead, and a good time for clearer divination.

III

The Old Ways

Five ways the Irish celebrated

  1. Food for the ancestors
  2. Divination
  3. Costumes and sweets
  4. Carving root vegetables
  5. The fire festival

Food for the ancestors

Spirits were believed to walk the Earth this night, and would often revisit their homes.

  • Doors and windows were left open for the ancestors.
  • Fires and candles were lit so the spirits could find their way home.
  • A plate of food was left out for them on the table, the fireplace, or an altar.

Divination

With the veil believed to be gone entirely, divination was thought to be much clearer to interpret.

Most of it centered on two things: death and marriage.

Apples

Apple bobbing
The first person to grab an apple with their teeth would be the next to marry.
Apple peels
Throw the peel over your shoulder, and it curls into the first letter of the name of the person you’ll marry.

Chestnuts, eggs, and smoke

Roasted chestnuts
One chestnut stood for you, the other for the person you wished to marry. If they popped away from each other, it was not a good sign. If they stayed toasty side by side, it was.
Egg white readings
The number of shapes told you how many children you would have.
The Druids
They would scry, cast lots, and interpret the smoke of the fires.

Costumes and sweets

  • Costumes were worn to confuse, frighten, and blend in with the fairies, monsters, and trickster spirits.
  • They also protected the wearer from being taken by the fairies, and eventually, the witches.
  • Treats were left out as a bribe for the cunning spirits.

Carving root vegetables

In Ireland, turnips were carved with faces, set in windows, and carried as lanterns.

The faces were meant to keep faeries, monsters, and unsettled spirits away, especially because of the old Irish folklore of Stingy Jack.

Stingy Jack

Tricked the devil for his own monetary gain.

Both God and the Devil kicked him out of Heaven and Hell, so he roams the Earth forever.

From turnips to pumpkins

Irish immigrants brought turnip carving to America in the 19th century.

They found pumpkins much easier to carve, and that’s where our tradition of carving pumpkins comes from.

The fire festival

Held on the first frost after the first full moon in October.

  1. Families let their hearth fires burn all the way out.
  2. They went into town and watched the Druids light a sacred fire.
  3. Food offerings were left at the edge of the fields for the faeries and spirits.
  4. A black sheep, black sow, or cow was sacrificed.
  5. Each family took home something lit by the sacred fire to relight their hearth, start bonfires, and light torches around their fields.
IV

Appalachian Traditions

How to apply them to your Samhain celebration

The silent supper

  • Host a dinner where you invite your ancestors, or the deceased you wish to honor and pay respect to.
  • Silence, or quiet conversation, shows respect for the dead.
  • If you do talk, center it on stories of those you’ve invited in.

Set their table

Make a meal your family and ancestors made.

Supper

  • Soup beans
  • Cornbread
  • Collard greens
  • The last fresh vegetables of the harvest
  • Fried taters
  • Coleslaw
  • Chow-chow

Coffee and moonshine

Ghost stories

Storytelling is a huge part of Appalachian culture, and ghost stories are a good way to scare adults and children alike.

  • Learn about your local haunts and share the stories told about them.
  • Ask the elders in your family if they know any ghost stories, or ones about the towns they live in.

Water bowl scrying

Tea leaf readings
Brew a cup with loose leaf tea for the person being read to drink. When the cup is empty, read the leaves. Different shapes mean different things.
